# Why This Idea

**Summary**: Refilling cancelled appointments from a waitlist is a solved, crowded product category in the United States. This document answers the question a judge will ask first, why build it again, with the evidence gathered for Puerto Rico: the work exists and is done by hand, the US products cannot enter Puerto Rico offices through the door they use, nobody local refills a cancelled slot with the next waiting patient, the offices are too small for the US vendors' economics, many offices run turns rather than timed slots, and the plans that carry the access standards do not measure them. It ends with the one-paragraph answer, the Q&A table, and the limits of the evidence.
**Sources**: the research pages in `wiki/` ([index](wiki/index.md)); every figure below carries its page and its label there.
**Last updated**: 2026-10-09

---

## 1. The question

The team knew the category exists before choosing the idea: Epic's Fast Pass, Luma Health, Phreesia, Relatient, NexHealth, Weave, Clearwave and Solutionreach all offer some form of cancelled-slot backfill, and two Puerto Rico companies, BrainHi and Citamed, already hold the schedule connection and the patient messaging ([wiki/idea-3-competitors.md](wiki/idea-3-competitors.md)). So the choice has to rest on a specific claim: **in Puerto Rico this work is still done by hand, the existing products do not reach the offices that do it, and a product built for the island's conditions can.** The sections below give the evidence for each part of that claim and what it changes in the design.

## 3. What the evidence changes in the product

- **The differentiator is the entry point, not the algorithm.** The queue starts from a cancellation the front desk confirms, keeps its own record, and needs no EHR integration. In the United States that is the degraded mode; in Puerto Rico it is the version that works. It also fits the chosen stack (local model, SQLite, no hosting cost): a two-person office can run it.
- **The unit is the next patient to be seen, not the 2pm slot.** A virtual queue ("fila virtual", the Senate's own term) serves the office that books by the hour and the office that cites by turn, and refills cancellations in both. The demo keeps timed slots, which is what is built; the pitch says the engine is a queue and the slot is one case of it. This is a team decision to confirm, not a change made here.
- **Phase 2 has a name and a date.** The queue across a plan's network answers a standard the regulator is not testing and will be obliged to test from 2027 to 2028. It is presented as planned, which the rules allow, with the single-office demo as the working part.
- **Messages are single-segment Spanish SMS or voice, with a delivery receipt before a slot is given away.** Small, and it shows the team knows the carriers it would run on.
- **Insurer is a label, never a selection criterion**, and offers are sequential, by the rules in the technical proposal.

## 4. The one-paragraph answer

**English.** Refilling cancelled appointments is solved in the United States, inside Epic and inside EHR-integrated suites. Puerto Rico's offices cannot use them: none of those products integrates a Puerto Rico record system, no Puerto Rico record system exposes its schedule, three in four offices have fewer than five people, and many still see patients by turn. So today that work is done by a secretary at thirteen dollars an hour with a list, nobody local sells the refill, and the plans that carry the access standards do not measure them. We built the version that fits that reality: it starts when the office confirms a cancellation, needs no integration, speaks by SMS and voice in Spanish sized for the island's carriers, works for turns as well as timed slots, never ranks by insurer because the law forbids it, and records every move. The queue across a plan's network is phase two, and it has a buyer and a date.

**Spanish.** Rellenar cancelaciones está resuelto en Estados Unidos, dentro de Epic y de suites integradas al EHR. Las oficinas de Puerto Rico no pueden usarlas: ninguna integra un sistema de aquí, ningún sistema de aquí expone su agenda, tres de cada cuatro oficinas tienen menos de cinco personas, y muchas todavía citan por turno. Así que hoy ese trabajo lo hace una secretaria a trece dólares la hora con una lista, nadie local vende el relleno, y los planes que cargan los estándares de acceso no los miden. Construimos la versión que cabe en esa realidad: arranca cuando la oficina confirma una cancelación, no necesita integración, habla por SMS y voz en español dimensionados para los carriers de aquí, funciona para turnos y para citas por hora, no ordena por aseguradora porque la ley lo prohíbe, y deja constancia de cada movimiento. La cola a nivel de plan es la fase dos, y tiene comprador y fecha.

## 5. Questions a judge may ask, and the honest answers

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| "BrainHi could add this in a sprint." | Yes. That is why the differentiator is the queue without integration and the plan-level phase, and why BrainHi is also a possible partner or buyer, not only a competitor. |
| "How many offices did you consult?" | Three: a cardiologist, a dental office, a primary-care practice. They agree with each other and with the public record. It is a signal, not a study. |
| "What is the no-show rate in Puerto Rico?" | No general measure exists. The only measured dataset (CFSE, workers' compensation) records about 15.6% of resolved appointments cancelled and 4.7% not attended. The cancellation is the slot a queue can refill. |
| "What if the office cites everyone at 8am?" | Then there is no slot to refill, and the virtual queue is what serves it. That is why the unit is the next patient, not the slot. |
| "Who pays?" | Today, the office paid per visit (specialists, dentists, therapy, imaging); the capitated primary-care office gains time, not revenue. Later, the plan, which has a standard, a Star measure and a regulatory clock. |
| "Does backfill work?" | Where it has been measured, modestly: 25 to 39% of offered slots filled, 3 to 14% of offers accepted, patients seen about two weeks sooner (median). Same-day offers convert worst. |
| "Why not WhatsApp?" | Meta's own terms exclude it for health information; no Puerto Rico office found uses it for booking; SMS and voice through a provider that signs a business associate agreement are the defensible channels. |
| "Is any of this already law?" | No. The 2026 Senate bill received a negative committee report. Its text is still the best written description of what an appointment system here should contain: reminders, waitlists, open slots, buffers, delay notices. |

## 6. Limits of the evidence

- The three office conversations were informal, without notes. A structured round (specialists, dentists, an office that uses BrainHi or Citamed) is the next step after the event.
- Absence is "not found": no document from a medical group or MSO mandating office software was found; no referral validity window was found; a plan-internal tool could exist without public coverage.
- The 15 to 30% no-show figure is a physicians' group claim in a legislative record, not a measurement; the $150 billion and $200 per visit figures are vendor claims and are not used.
- Mayo's "24.6% of offers accepted" does not fit its own offer counts and is quoted conservatively until the paper is re-read ([waitlist-backfill](wiki/waitlist-backfill.md)).
